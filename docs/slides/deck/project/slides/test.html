Five predictions, written down before any run

One link is often not enough (H1) · today’s schedulers waste capacity when delays differ (H2) · our scheduler freezes less (H3) · it needs no special protocol (H4) · it helps most with big keyframes (H5)

Be precise about the connections. Today every run uses two emulated links on one laptop: two network namespaces joined by two virtual links, each with its own speed, delay and 200 ms buffer, set by Linux traffic control. Scenarios: both 1.6 Mbit/s at 40 ms; 1.6 Mbit/s at 30 and 150 ms; 2.0 and 1.2 Mbit/s at 40 and 60 ms; 1.8 Mbit/s each with each link dropping out for 2 seconds in turn every 15 seconds; and a 4 Mbit/s control. No phones are involved yet. To check that the emulator follows a changing rate faithfully, we replayed two public uplink recordings from the Mahimahi project (AT&T LTE and T-Mobile UMTS, recorded while driving in the US); those are for calibration only, not results. Next, teammates record two Android phones on two different Indian operators at the same time and place, at three sites (indoor campus, outdoor, moving vehicle), sending to a small cloud server; we replay those recordings as the real_pairs scenario. A frame counts as shown only if it and every frame it depends on arrived within 250 ms of capture; a freeze is a gap of at least 190 ms. Each comparison uses 8 paired runs: with 6 main comparisons, Holm's first threshold is 0.05/6 = 0.0083, and 8 runs is the fewest whose best Wilcoxon p-value (2/2^8 = 0.0078) can pass it.
